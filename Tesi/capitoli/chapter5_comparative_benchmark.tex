\chapter{Comparative Benchmark}
\label{chap:comparative_benchmark}

Having formalized the mathematical optimization framework in Chapter~\ref{chap:ilp_architecture} and established the calibrated operational parameters in Chapter~\ref{chap:performance_analysis}, this chapter presents an extensive comparative performance evaluation of the proposed Centralized Integer Linear Programming (ILP) Orchestrator against state-of-the-art distributed heuristics and hierarchical optimization baselines~\cite{icdcs_satellite_2025}.

Deploying orbital edge intelligence across Low Earth Orbit (LEO) constellations involves balancing competing architectural paradigms~\cite{denby2019orbital, denby2020asplos, survey_orbital_edge_2023}. On the one hand, decentralized heuristics provide rapid, low-complexity decisions with negligible queuing overhead~\cite{icdcs_satellite_2025}. On the other hand, optimization models search the joint combinatorial space of compute allocation and multi-hop Inter-Satellite Link (ISL) routing to achieve theoretically optimal resource utilization~\cite{wolsey1998integer, zhong2025leo}.

To quantify the operational trade-offs between these paradigms, this benchmark evaluates 9 algorithm configurations across a comprehensive testing matrix spanning diverse workload profiles, real-time payload dimensions, initial battery energy reserves, and request arrival rates.

\section{Experimental Setup and Baseline Algorithms}
\label{sec:bench_setup}

The comparative evaluation is conducted within the event-driven discrete-event simulation framework developed in Python with \texttt{SimPy}~\cite{simpy2023}, backed by scientific computing routines in \texttt{NumPy} and \texttt{SciPy}~\cite{harris2020numpy, virtanen2020scipy}. The simulation faithfully reproduces satellite orbital kinematics using SGP4 ephemeris propagation~\cite{vallado2006sgp4}, dynamic line-of-sight visibility windows, optical ISL transceivers~\cite{chen2021isl, chaudhry2021laser}, processor queues, and onboard battery charge-discharge cycles.

\subsection{Benchmark Dimensioning and Scenario Parameters}
\label{subsec:bench_parameters}

The orbital network is configured as a regional Walker-Delta LEO constellation~\cite{walker1984satellite} operating at an orbital altitude of $600\text{ km}$. Adjacent satellites are interconnected via full-duplex optical ISLs~\cite{chen2021isl, chaudhry2021laser} characterized by nominal transmission bandwidths $B_{u,v}$ and dynamically computed propagation latencies. Computational requests generated by Ground Users (GUs) are ingested through regional Access Points ($AP_{global}$) and forwarded to candidate Satellite Edge Nodes (SENs)~\cite{leyva2023satellite, icdcs_satellite_2025}.

The benchmark explores a multi-dimensional parameter space:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Workload Distribution Vector $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (\beta_G, \beta_C, \beta_{CD})$:} Represents the proportion of Generic tasks, CPU-intensive tasks, and CPU \& Data-intensive tasks. We systematically evaluate three distinct operational regimes:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textit{Mixed Multi-Service Profile:} $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0.40, 0.35, 0.25)$, representing a balanced heterogeneous mission.
        \item \textit{Computation-Heavy Profile:} $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0, 1, 0)$, isolating processor clock cycles stress with minimal network footprint.
        \item \textit{Communication-Heavy Profile (Data-Intensive):} $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0, 0, 1)$, exerting concurrent stress on onboard CPU cores, ISL bandwidth, and transceiver transmission energy.
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Real-Time Payload Vector $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\alpha_M, \alpha_L, \alpha_V)$:} Governs the distribution of input/output data payload sizes across Medium ($1\text{--}5\text{ MB}$), Large ($5\text{--}20\text{ MB}$), and Very Large ($20\text{--}50\text{ MB}$) files. Four sub-profiles are benchmarked for every scenario to evaluate the impact of link saturation:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textit{Standard (Std):} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0.30, 0.50, 0.20)$.
        \item \textit{Large-heavy (L-heavy):} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0, 0.70, 0.30)$.
        \item \textit{Very-Large-heavy (VL-heavy):} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0, 0.30, 0.70)$.
        \item \textit{Pure Large (Pure L):} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0, 1.0, 0)$.
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Fleet Operational Energy Budget ($E_{i}$):} Candidate SENs are initialized with usable battery energy reserves of either $40\text{ kJ}$ (stringent energy constraint) or $80\text{ kJ}$ (relaxed energy constraint) per orbital pass.

    \item \textbf{Traffic Intensity Spectrum ($\lambda$):} Request arrival rates vary systematically across $\lambda \in \{2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10\}\text{ req/s}$, transitioning the network from a low-load regime to a heavy-load saturation regime.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluated Algorithms and State-of-the-Art Baselines}
\label{subsec:bench_algorithms}

The comparative suite comprises 9 algorithmic configurations categorized into three architectural paradigms:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Distributed Heuristics:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{\texttt{DTS-base}:} A greedy distributed baseline that offloads tasks to the geographically closest available SEN possessing sufficient idle CPU capacity~\cite{icdcs_satellite_2025}.
        \item \textbf{\texttt{DTS-Optimal}:} An enhanced multi-criteria greedy heuristic jointly evaluating candidate processing queues and initial access-point link bandwidth~\cite{icdcs_satellite_2025}.
        \item \textbf{\texttt{OrbitAware}:} A trajectory-predictive distributed heuristic leveraging orbital ephemeris~\cite{vallado2006sgp4} to compute shortest paths via Dijkstra's algorithm~\cite{dijkstra1959note} and balance computational load across candidate orbital planes~\cite{icdcs_satellite_2025}.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Distributed / Hierarchical Optimization Models:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{\texttt{ILP-Hierarchical (Time)}:} A two-stage mathematical optimization formulation solved locally within satellite clusters, prioritizing task response time~\cite{icdcs_satellite_2025}.
        \item \textbf{\texttt{ILP-Hierarchical (Energy)}:} A distributed formulation prioritizing constellation energy preservation~\cite{icdcs_satellite_2025}.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Proposed Centralized ILP Orchestrator:}
    Formulated under Integer Linear Programming principles~\cite{wolsey1998integer} using \texttt{PuLP}~\cite{pulp2011} and solved via exact dual simplex solvers~\cite{huangfu2018highs}. Following the micro-tuning calibration established in Chapter~\ref{chap:performance_analysis} (which identified the calibrated objective function weighting parameters as $\alpha^* = 1000$ and $\gamma^* = 10$, balancing primary task admission against secondary operational overhead), the orchestrator is evaluated under optimal weights across two viable batching archetypes:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{\texttt{ILP-Centr (BS20, BT0.05, Time/Energy)}:} Reactive fast-dispatch configuration with an aggressive $50\text{ ms}$ timeout.
        \item \textbf{\texttt{ILP-Centr (BS2, BT2, Time/Energy)}:} Controlled small-batching policy ($BS=2$, $BT=2\text{ s}$) enabling pairwise combinatorial optimization.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Comparative Performance Evaluation across Workload Profiles}
\label{sec:comparative_evaluation}

We evaluate the algorithms across three primary performance metrics:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Task Success Rate (\%):} The fraction of total offloaded requests successfully routed, executed, and delivered within the deadline $D_r$ without violating battery limits.
    \item \textbf{End-to-End Response Time (s):} Decomposed into \textit{Onboard Task Execution Time} ($T_{exec}$, capturing physical processing of the task on the destination SEN CPU cores) and \textit{Network/Wait Time} ($T_{net+wait}$, capturing batch queuing at the orchestrator, multi-hop ISL propagation, and payload transmission).
    \item \textbf{Rejection Causes Breakdown (\%):} The diagnostic distribution of task drops categorized into \textit{Deadline Exceeded}, \textit{Insufficient CPU+NET}, \textit{Insufficient CPU}, \textit{No Server Found}, \textit{ILP Infeasible}, and \textit{Sunset}.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:sr_mixed_40kJ} and Figure~\ref{fig:sr_mixed_80kJ} illustrate the task completion rate across the arrival rate spectrum $\lambda \in [2, 10]\text{ req/s}$ under $40\text{ kJ}$ and $80\text{ kJ}$ battery budgets, evaluated across the four payload profiles $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$.

Under light traffic ($\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$), distributed heuristics (\texttt{DTS-base}, \texttt{DTS-Optimal}, and \texttt{OrbitAware}) achieve near-optimal throughput, delivering completion rates of $94\text{--}95\%$ at $40\text{ kJ}$ and climbing to $98\text{--}99\%$ when the battery budget is expanded to $80\text{ kJ}$. As traffic escalates to the saturation threshold ($\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$), these heuristics sustain completion rates between $79\%$ and $83\%$. Their localized greedy decision-making bypasses queuing bottlenecks entirely, allowing task admissions to scale directly with instantaneous physical resource availability.

In contrast, mathematical optimization formulations exhibit substantial performance divergence governed by batching parameters and objective functions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Small-Batching Resilience under Time Mapping:} The centralized small-batch configuration with latency optimization (\texttt{ILP-Centr [BS2, BT2, Time]}) consistently achieves the highest throughput among all optimization-based approaches across all arrival rates. It starts at $82\text{--}84\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) and $90\text{--}92\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$) at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$, and maintains $54\text{--}56\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) and $65\text{--}67\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$) even at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$. In contrast, its energy-optimizing counterpart (\texttt{ILP-Centr [BS2, BT2, Energy]}) suffers severe degradation, achieving $50\text{--}53\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) / $59\text{--}63\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$) at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$ and declining to $29\text{--}33\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) / $35\text{--}38\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$) at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$. Prioritizing energy minimization penalizes forwarding over multi-hop topologies, rejecting tasks that would otherwise be schedulable under time minimization.
    \item \textbf{Fast-Dispatch Performance:} The centralized fast-dispatch configurations (\texttt{BS20, BT0.05}) provide competitive throughput at low load, with Time mapping achieving $74\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) / $79\text{--}80\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$) and Energy mapping reaching $67\text{--}69\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) / $72\text{--}75\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$). However, under peak load ($\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$), they degrade to $42\text{--}43\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) / $46\text{--}47\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$) for Time, and $39\text{--}41\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) / $42\text{--}45\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$) for Energy. While bounding the timeout to $BT = 0.05\text{ s}$ prevents ingress queue accumulation, it falls short of \texttt{ILP-Centr [BS2, BT2, Time]} by $12\text{--}20\%$ at high load, because pairwise combinatorial optimization resolves link contention far more effectively than fast dispatch.
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical Throughput Collapse:} The hierarchical formulations (\texttt{ILP-Hierarchical} for both Time and Energy) exhibit an acute inflection point: while achieving $93\text{--}95\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) and $97\text{--}99\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$) at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$, their completion rates drop precipitously once traffic exceeds $\lambda = 3\text{ req/s}$, bottoming out at $36\text{--}43\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) and $39\text{--}45\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$) at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$. The two-tier coordination overhead and inter-cluster aggregation delays introduce structural latency that severely compromises task feasibility during traffic surges.
\end{itemize}

Across payload distributions, shifting from \textit{Standard} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0.3, 0.5, 0.2)$ to \textit{VL-heavy} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0, 0.3, 0.7)$ or \textit{Pure L} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0, 1, 0)$ systematically depresses completion rates by $2\text{--}5\%$ across all algorithms due to increased serialization delay over optical ISLs. Doubling the usable battery capacity from $40\text{ kJ}$ to $80\text{ kJ}$ provides a uniform upward shift of $+4\%$ to $+12\%$, with the most pronounced benefit observed in multi-hop optimization configurations.

Figure~\ref{fig:rt_mixed_40kJ} and Figure~\ref{fig:rt_mixed_80kJ} present the end-to-end response time decomposed into physical Onboard Task Execution Time ($T_{exec}$, solid lower bar) and Network/Queuing Delay ($T_{net+wait}$, opaque upper bar), measured in seconds ($\text{s}$).

The empirical results demonstrate a clear architectural bifurcation in latency dynamics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heuristic Invariance:} The distributed heuristics maintain remarkably stable response times across all traffic rates, bounded tightly between $0.70\text{ s}$ and $0.80\text{ s}$ under \textit{Std} and reaching at most $0.85\text{--}0.86\text{ s}$ under \textit{VL-heavy}. Crucially, $T_{exec}$ remains invariant at $0.30\text{--}0.34\text{ s}$, while $T_{net+wait}$ accounts for $0.40\text{--}0.52\text{ s}$. Because distributed heuristics execute immediate local forwarding without ingress buffering queues, their network delay reflects pure physical ISL propagation and transmission time.
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical Latency Inflation:} \texttt{ILP-Hierarchical} models suffer substantial latency expansion under load. Mean response times rise from $0.80\text{--}0.85\text{ s}$ (reaching $0.93\text{ s}$ under \textit{VL-heavy}) at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$ to $1.10\text{--}1.28\text{ s}$ at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$ (peaking at $1.28\text{ s}$ under $80\text{ kJ}$ \textit{VL-heavy}). The decomposition reveals that while onboard task processing time on the destination SEN ($T_{exec}$) increases from $0.33\text{ s}$ to $0.48\text{--}0.52\text{ s}$ under concurrent core utilization, the primary growth driver is $T_{net+wait}$ (expanding to $0.70\text{--}0.76\text{ s}$), reflecting acute queuing backlog during hierarchical task aggregation.
    \item \textbf{Low-Latency Bounds of Fast Dispatch:} The centralized fast-dispatch models (\texttt{BS20, BT0.05}) achieve the lowest baseline response times in the entire benchmark, recording $0.50\text{--}0.54\text{ s}$ for Time (bar~[6]) and $0.42\text{--}0.48\text{ s}$ for Energy (bar~[7]) at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$. Under peak load ($\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$), response time expands moderately to $0.65\text{--}0.75\text{ s}$ (Time) and $0.60\text{--}0.65\text{ s}$ (Energy). Their onboard execution footprint is exceptionally low ($T_{exec} \le 0.26\text{ s}$), while capping the batch timeout at $0.05\text{ s}$ strictly suppresses ingress queuing accumulation.
    \item \textbf{Small-Batch Dynamics:} For \texttt{ILP-Centr [BS2, BT2, Time]} (bar~[8]), response time starts slightly higher at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$ ($0.72\text{--}0.76\text{ s}$) due to queuing dwell times awaiting batch capacity or timeout trigger ($T_{net+wait} \approx 0.50\text{--}0.53\text{ s}$, $T_{exec} \approx 0.21\text{--}0.23\text{ s}$). However, as traffic scales to $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$, response time decreases and stabilizes at $0.56\text{--}0.65\text{ s}$ across all payload profiles, because the small batch threshold of 2 is satisfied almost instantaneously by incoming traffic bursts. Meanwhile, \texttt{ILP-Centr [BS2, BT2, Energy]} (bar~[9]) exhibits low latency ($0.45\text{--}0.63\text{ s}$, with $T_{exec} \approx 0.17\text{--}0.23\text{ s}$) because it aggressively drops tasks requiring multi-hop routing, admitting predominantly single-hop assignments.
\end{itemize}

\end{itemize}

In contrast to payload invariance, expanding the fleet battery reserves from $40\text{ kJ}$ to $80\text{ kJ}$ provides a noticeable throughput gain across all schemes ($+4\%$ to $+11\%$), driven by the energy consumed by onboard CPU core executions during extended simulation intervals.

Under these operating conditions, a marked performance differentiation is observed among algorithmic paradigms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Resilience of Distributed Heuristics:} The heuristic algorithms (\texttt{DTS-base}, \texttt{DTS-Optimal}, and \texttt{OrbitAware}) demonstrate remarkable robustness, recording completion rates of $88\text{--}89\%$ at light load ($\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$) under $40\text{ kJ}$ (climbing to $94\text{--}95\%$ at $80\text{ kJ}$) and degrading gradually to $72\text{--}74\%$ at $40\text{ kJ}$ ($76\text{--}77\%$ at $80\text{ kJ}$) at peak load ($\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$). Their greedy evaluation without upstream queuing allows them to saturate physical SEN core capacity directly.

Multi-tier aggregation delays render the hierarchical paradigm inadequate for dense computational workloads.
    \item \textbf{Superiority of Small-Batching over Fast-Dispatch:} The small-batching configuration with latency optimization (\texttt{ILP-Centr [BS2, BT2, Time]}) demonstrates superior resilience under computational stress, sustaining a completion rate of $46\text{--}47\%$ at $40\text{ kJ}$ and $56\text{--}58\%$ at $80\text{ kJ}$ at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$. Centralized fast-dispatch (\texttt{ILP-Centr [BS20, BT0.05, Time/Energy]}) achieves $75\text{--}77\%$ at $40\text{ kJ}$ and $84\text{--}85\%$ at $80\text{ kJ}$ under light load, but degrades steeply to $29\text{--}30\%$ at $40\text{ kJ}$ and $31\text{--}33\%$ at $80\text{ kJ}$ under full load. Small-batching out-performs fast-dispatch by up to $+25\%$ at peak load. Furthermore, \texttt{ILP-Centr [BS2, BT2, Energy]} starts at $54\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) / $60\text{--}61\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$) at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$ and sustains $37\text{--}38\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) / $47\text{--}48\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$) at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$, also exceeding fast-dispatch throughput under high traffic.
\end{itemize}

\section{Trade-Off Analysis and Operational Discussion}
\label{sec:bench_tradeoffs}

Correlating the empirical findings across the evaluated scenarios reveals three foundational operational trade-offs governing satellite task offloading.

\subsection{Theoretical Global Optimality vs. Ingress Queuing Latency}
\label{subsec:tradeoff_optimality_queuing}

The fundamental architectural dilemma of satellite orchestration lies in the trade-off between global combinatorial scheduling efficiency and ingress queuing latency:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Distributed Heuristics:} Operate on an immediate dispatching model with zero buffer aggregation. Requests arriving at an Access Point are evaluated instantaneously against local node states. This reactive posture yields exceptional resilience against heavy request arrival rates ($\lambda \ge 6\text{ req/s}$), preventing deadline expiration almost entirely ($<2\%$ at high load). However, lacking global topological visibility, greedy localized forwarding induces inter-plane link saturation and transceiver depletion in data-intensive regimes.
    \item \textbf{Centralized Mathematical Optimization:} Solves an exact Mixed-Integer Linear Program over the constellation network~\cite{wolsey1998integer, boyd2004convex}. In compute-intensive and mixed regimes under Time mapping, configurations utilizing extended batch buffers ($BS = 20$) incur queuing delays that erode task deadline slack:
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta t_{slack} = D_r - (T_{wait} + T_{up} + W_{cpu,i} + T_{exec} + T_{down})
    \end{equation}
    When $\Delta t_{slack} < 0$, tasks expire inside the orchestrator queue before assignment can occur, causing high \textit{Deadline Exceeded} drops ($62\%\text{--}80\%$ in fast-dispatch under peak load, and up to $82\%\text{--}90\%$ in hierarchical models). In contrast, small-batching orchestrators ($BS = 2$) almost entirely avoid buffer dwell latency under heavy traffic, driving deadline drop rates down to $<2\%$ in Mixed and $8\%\text{--}14\%$ in CPU-intensive profiles, shifting the operational failure boundary decisively back to physical compute exhaustion.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Energy Budget Sensitivity Across Dimensional Boundaries}
\label{subsec:tradeoff_energy_sensitivity}

The empirical evaluation demonstrates that fleet battery sizing ($40\text{ kJ}$ vs. $80\text{ kJ}$) exerts an asymmetric impact depending on workload dimensionality:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Compute-Bound Regimes ($\boldsymbol{\beta}=(0, 1, 0)$):} 
    Shows complete invariance across payload size distributions $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$. Expanding battery capacity from $40\text{ kJ}$ to $80\text{ kJ}$ yields a moderate throughput increase ($+4\%\text{--}11\%$) across algorithms due to onboard CPU core energy consumption, but cannot resolve deadline expirations caused by queuing delays in large-batch setups.
    \item \textbf{Communication-Bound Regimes ($\boldsymbol{\beta}=(0, 0, 1)$):} 
    Transmitting large payload files ($20\text{--}50\text{ MB}$) across multi-hop ISL paths demands substantial transceiver power~\cite{chen2021isl}. Expanding battery reserves to $80\text{ kJ}$ unlocks throughput gains of $+4\%$ for heuristics, $+4\%\text{--}8\%$ for centralized models, and up to $+9\%\text{--}15\%$ for hierarchical optimization at peak load.
    \item \textbf{Mixed Multi-Service Regimes ($\boldsymbol{\beta}=(0.40, 0.35, 0.25)$):} 
    Heterogeneous workloads exhibit intermediate sensitivity ($+4\%\text{--}12\%$ throughput improvement under $80\text{ kJ}$), with benefits concentrating primarily in optimization models that leverage multi-hop ISL routing.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Fast-Dispatch vs. Controlled Small-Batching Orchestration}
\label{subsec:tradeoff_dispatch_policies}

Benchmarking the two tuned centralized optimization archetypes provides clear design boundaries regarding queue management:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Centralized Small-Batching (\texttt{BS2, BT2, Time}):} 
    Consistently achieves the highest task completion rate among centralized schemes across all arrival rates and workload profiles ($54\text{--}67\%$ in Mixed, $46\text{--}58\%$ in CPU-intensive, and $59\text{--}68\%$ in Data-intensive at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$). Under heavy load, the batch capacity of 2 fills almost instantaneously, eliminating buffer dwell time while enabling pairwise combinatorial scheduling that resolves resource contention far more effectively than single-task dispatch.
    \item \textbf{Centralized Fast-Dispatch (\texttt{BS20, BT0.05, Time}):} 
    Enforcing an aggressive timeout ($BT = 0.05\text{ s}$) maintains the lowest baseline latency under light load ($0.50\text{--}0.54\text{ s}$ in Mixed, $0.68\text{--}0.75\text{ s}$ in CPU, $0.85\text{--}1.05\text{ s}$ in Data). However, under heavy load, ingress queuing backlogs accumulate, causing it to admit fewer requests than small-batching ($42\text{--}47\%$ in Mixed and $29\text{--}33\%$ in CPU-intensive, trailing small-batching by $12\%\text{--}25\%$). This makes fast-dispatch suitable primarily when strict low response latency under moderate traffic is prioritized over completion volume under saturation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary of Chapter Findings}
\label{sec:bench_summary}

The comprehensive comparative benchmark across distributed heuristics, hierarchical ILP, and tuned centralized orchestrators establishes five definitive findings:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Isolation of the Temporal vs. Physical Bottleneck:} 
    In compute-heavy workloads ($\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0, 1, 0)$), failure profiles reveal an architectural batching split rather than an objective-driven one. Distributed heuristics fail strictly due to physical processor saturation ($100\%$ \textit{Insufficient CPU}, $0\%$ \textit{Deadline Exceeded}). Centralized configurations with larger batch thresholds ($BS = 20$, bars~[6] and~[7]) as well as hierarchical formulations suffer extensive deadline expirations ($62\%\text{--}78\%$ in fast-dispatch, and $82\%\text{--}90\%$ in hierarchical models at peak load) under sustained load, as ingress buffer dwell times deplete task slack. In sharp contrast, small-batching orchestrators ($BS = 2$, bars~[8] and~[9]) flush requests nearly instantaneously, shifting $86\%\text{--}92\%$ of rejections back to physical core saturation (\textit{Insufficient CPU}) and restricting deadline violations to just $8\%\text{--}14\%$. When transitioning to communication-heavy workloads ($\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0, 0, 1)$), this physical bottleneck shifts completely from CPU exhaustion ($0\%$ \textit{Insufficient CPU}) to transceiver and link capacity ($100\%$ \textit{Insufficient CPU+NET} for heuristics, and $>95\text{--}100\%$ for optimization baselines).

    \item \textbf{Dimensional Asymmetry of Energy Sensitivity:} 
    Workload dimensionality governs sensitivity to satellite battery capacity. Compute-bound workloads ($\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0, 1, 0)$) are completely invariant to payload size distributions $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$, while benefiting moderately ($+4\%\text{--}11\%$) from larger battery capacity due to CPU power draw. Conversely, communication-heavy workloads ($\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0, 0, 1)$) exhibit high elasticity, where doubling onboard battery capacity recovers up to $+9\%\text{--}15\%$ throughput in hierarchical models and $+4\%\text{--}8\%$ in centralized configurations by sustaining transceiver power across multi-hop optical paths.

    \item \textbf{Algorithmic Inversion in Data-Intensive Regimes:} 
    Optimization models are not universally inferior to heuristics under heavy load. In data-intensive scenarios, \texttt{ILP-Hierarchical (Time)} decisively outperforms distributed heuristics ($99\text{--}100\%$ vs. $94\text{--}95\%$ at low load, and $81\text{--}89\%$ vs. $77\text{--}78\%$ at peak load under $80\text{ kJ}$). 

    \item \textbf{Bounded Nature of Physical Execution Time ($T_{exec}$):} 
    Across all evaluated algorithms, arrival rates, and workload profiles, the physical processing time on destination SEN cores remains tightly bounded ($T_{exec} \approx 0.17\text{--}0.48\text{ s}$ across heuristics, fast-dispatch, and small-batching, reaching at most $0.52\text{ s}$ in hierarchical models under mixed load and $0.65\text{ s}$ under data-intensive \textit{VL-heavy}). End-to-end latency expansion is driven primarily by ingress queuing, cluster aggregation delays, and optical transmission serialization ($T_{net+wait}$).

    \item \textbf{Marginalization of Eclipse Interruptions:} 
    Task rejections due to orbital dusk (\textit{Sunset}) are completely eradicated across all distributed heuristics and throughout the entire data-intensive regime ($0.0\%$), and remain strictly marginalized ($<2\text{--}5\%$) in centralized optimization models under light load ($\lambda \le 4\text{ req/s}$) where tasks dwell in queues across orbital plane visibility transitions. This confirms that predictive orbital ephemeris awareness effectively shields offloaded tasks from line-of-sight eclipse drops.
\end{enumerate}

These empirical findings rigorously define the operational boundaries of centralized orbital orchestration, establishing the foundation for the concluding assessments and future research directions presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusions}.
