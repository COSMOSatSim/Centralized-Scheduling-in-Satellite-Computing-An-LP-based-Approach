\chapter{Comparative Benchmark}
\label{chap:comparative_benchmark}

Having formalized the mathematical optimization framework in Chapter~\ref{chap:ilp_architecture} and established the calibrated operational parameters in Chapter~\ref{chap:performance_analysis}, this chapter presents an extensive comparative performance evaluation of the proposed Centralized Integer Linear Programming (ILP) Orchestrator against state-of-the-art distributed heuristics and hierarchical optimization baselines~\cite{icdcs_satellite_2025}.

Deploying orbital edge intelligence across Low Earth Orbit (LEO) constellations involves balancing competing architectural paradigms. On the one hand, decentralized heuristics provide rapid, low-complexity decisions with negligible queuing overhead. On the other hand, optimization models search the joint combinatorial space of compute allocation and multi-hop Inter-Satellite Link (ISL) routing to achieve theoretically optimal resource utilization.

To quantify the operational trade-offs between these paradigms, this benchmark evaluates 9 algorithm configurations across a comprehensive testing matrix spanning diverse workload profiles, real-time payload dimensions, initial battery energy reserves, and request arrival rates.

\section{Experimental Setup and Baseline Algorithms}
\label{sec:bench_setup}

The comparative evaluation is conducted within the event-driven discrete-event simulation framework developed in Python with \texttt{SimPy}~\cite{simpy2023}. The simulation faithfully reproduces satellite orbital kinematics, dynamic line-of-sight visibility windows, optical ISL transceivers, processor queues, and onboard battery charge-discharge cycles.

\subsection{Benchmark Dimensioning and Scenario Parameters}
\label{subsec:bench_parameters}

The orbital network is configured as a regional Walker-Delta LEO constellation~\cite{walker1984satellite} operating at an orbital altitude of $600\text{ km}$. Adjacent satellites are interconnected via full-duplex optical ISLs~\cite{chaudhry2021laser} characterized by nominal transmission bandwidths $B_{u,v}$ and dynamically computed propagation latencies. Computational requests generated by Ground Users (GUs) are ingested through regional Access Points ($AP_{global}$) and forwarded to candidate Satellite Edge Nodes (SENs).

The benchmark explores a multi-dimensional parameter space:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Workload Distribution Vector $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (\beta_G, \beta_C, \beta_{CD})$:} Represents the proportion of Generic tasks, CPU-intensive tasks, and CPU \& Data-intensive tasks. We systematically evaluate three distinct operational regimes:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textit{Mixed Multi-Service Profile:} $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0.40, 0.35, 0.25)$, representing a balanced heterogeneous mission.
        \item \textit{Computation-Heavy Profile:} $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0, 1, 0)$, isolating processor clock cycles stress with minimal network footprint.
        \item \textit{Communication-Heavy Profile (Data-Intensive):} $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0, 0, 1)$, exerting concurrent stress on onboard CPU cores, ISL bandwidth, and transceiver transmission energy.
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Real-Time Payload Vector $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\alpha_M, \alpha_L, \alpha_V)$:} Governs the distribution of input/output data payload sizes across Medium ($1\text{--}5\text{ MB}$), Large ($5\text{--}20\text{ MB}$), and Very Large ($20\text{--}50\text{ MB}$) files. Four sub-profiles are benchmarked for every scenario to evaluate the impact of link saturation:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textit{Standard (Std):} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0.30, 0.50, 0.20)$.
        \item \textit{Large-heavy (L-heavy):} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0, 0.70, 0.30)$.
        \item \textit{Very-Large-heavy (VL-heavy):} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0, 0.30, 0.70)$.
        \item \textit{Pure Large (Pure L):} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0, 1.0, 0)$.
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Fleet Operational Energy Budget ($E_{i}$):} Candidate SENs are initialized with usable battery energy reserves of either $40\text{ kJ}$ (stringent energy constraint) or $80\text{ kJ}$ (relaxed energy constraint) per orbital pass.

    \item \textbf{Traffic Intensity Spectrum ($\lambda$):} Request arrival rates vary systematically across $\lambda \in \{2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10\}\text{ req/s}$, transitioning the network from a low-load regime to a heavy-load saturation regime.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluated Algorithms and State-of-the-Art Baselines}
\label{subsec:bench_algorithms}

The comparative suite comprises 9 algorithmic configurations categorized into three architectural paradigms:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Distributed Heuristics:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{\texttt{DTS-base}:} A greedy distributed baseline that offloads tasks to the geographically closest available SEN possessing sufficient idle CPU capacity~\cite{icdcs_satellite_2025}.
        \item \textbf{\texttt{DTS-Optimal}:} An enhanced multi-criteria greedy heuristic jointly evaluating candidate processing queues and initial access-point link bandwidth~\cite{icdcs_satellite_2025}.
        \item \textbf{\texttt{OrbitAware}:} A trajectory-predictive distributed heuristic leveraging orbital ephemeris to select shortest paths and balance computational load~\cite{icdcs_satellite_2025}.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Distributed / Hierarchical Optimization Models:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{\texttt{ILP-Hierarchical (Time)}:} A two-stage mathematical optimization formulation solved locally within satellite clusters, prioritizing task response time~\cite{icdcs_satellite_2025}.
        \item \textbf{\texttt{ILP-Hierarchical (Energy)}:} A distributed formulation prioritizing constellation energy preservation~\cite{icdcs_satellite_2025}.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Proposed Centralized ILP Orchestrator:}
    Following the micro-tuning calibration established in Chapter~\ref{chap:performance_analysis}, the centralized orchestrator is evaluated under optimal weights across two viable batching archetypes:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{\texttt{ILP-Centr (BS20, BT0.05, Time/Energy)}:} Reactive fast-dispatch configuration with a 50~ms timeout.
        \item \textbf{\texttt{ILP-Centr (BS2, BT2, Time/Energy)}:} Controlled small-batching policy ($BS=2$).
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Comparative Performance Evaluation across Workload Profiles}
\label{sec:comparative_evaluation}

We evaluate the algorithms across three primary performance metrics:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Task Success Rate (\%):} The fraction of total offloaded requests successfully routed, executed, and delivered within the deadline $D_r$ without violating battery limits.
    \item \textbf{End-to-End Response Time (ms):} Decomposed into \textit{Onboard Task Execution Time} ($T_{exec}$, capturing physical processing of the task on the destination SEN CPU cores) and \textit{Network/Wait Time} ($T_{net+wait}$, capturing batch queuing at the orchestrator, multi-hop ISL propagation, and payload transmission).
    \item \textbf{Rejection Causes Breakdown (\%):} The diagnostic distribution of task drops categorized into \textit{Deadline Exceeded}, \textit{Insufficient CPU+NET}, \textit{Insufficient CPU}, \textit{No Server Found}, \textit{ILP Infeasible}, and \textit{Sunset}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Success Rate Dynamics}
Figure~\ref{fig:sr_mixed} illustrates the task completion rate across the arrival rate spectrum $\lambda \in [2, 10]\text{ req/s}$ under $40\text{ kJ}$ and $80\text{ kJ}$ battery budgets, evaluated across the four payload profiles $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$.

Under light traffic ($\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$), distributed heuristics (\texttt{DTS-base}, \texttt{DTS-Optimal}, and \texttt{OrbitAware}) achieve near-optimal throughput, delivering completion rates of $94\text{--}95\%$ at $40\text{ kJ}$ and climbing to $98\text{--}99\%$ when the battery budget is expanded to $80\text{ kJ}$. As traffic escalates to the saturation threshold ($\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$), these heuristics demonstrate exceptional resilience, sustaining completion rates between $79\%$ and $84\%$. Their localized greedy decision-making bypasses queuing bottlenecks entirely, allowing task admissions to scale directly with instantaneous physical resource availability.

In contrast, mathematical optimization formulations exhibit substantial performance divergence governed by batching parameters and objective functions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Small-Batching Resilience under Time Mapping:} The centralized small-batch configuration with latency optimization (\texttt{ILP-Centr [BS2, BT2, Time]}) emerges as the top-performing optimization scheme. It achieves an initial completion rate of $84\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) and $91\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$) at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$, and gracefully degrades to $55\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) and $67\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$) at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$. In contrast, its energy-optimizing counterpart (\texttt{ILP-Centr [BS2, BT2, Energy]}) suffers severe degradation, achieving only $50\text{--}52\%$ at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$ and plunging to $28\text{--}38\%$ at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$. This reveals that under small batch sizes, prioritizing energy minimization diverts flows through multi-hop topologies that exceed tight deadline slacks.
    \item \textbf{Fast-Dispatch Stability:} The centralized fast-dispatch configuration (\texttt{ILP-Centr [BS20, BT0.05, Time]}) delivers consistent throughput, starting at $74\%$ ($40\text{ kJ}$) / $80\%$ ($80\text{ kJ}$) at low load and settling at $42\text{--}47\%$ under peak load. Restricting ingress buffering timeout to $BT = 0.05\text{ s}$ prevents the accumulation of stale tasks, outperforming energy-focused dispatch by $5\text{--}8\%$ across all arrival rates.
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical Throughput Collapse:} The hierarchical formulations (\texttt{ILP-Hierarchical} for both Time and Energy) exhibit an acute inflection point: while achieving $93\text{--}98\%$ at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$, their completion rates drop precipitously once traffic exceeds $\lambda = 3\text{ req/s}$, bottoming out at $36\text{--}44\%$ at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$. The two-tier coordination overhead and inter-cluster aggregation delays introduce structural latency that degrades task feasibility during traffic surges.
\end{itemize}

Across payload distributions, shifting from \textit{Standard} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0.3, 0.5, 0.2)$ to \textit{VL-heavy} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0, 0.3, 0.7)$ or \textit{Pure L} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0, 1, 0)$ systematically depresses completion rates by $2\text{--}5\%$ across all algorithms, driven by the increased serialization delay and transmission overhead over optical ISLs. Doubling the usable battery capacity from $40\text{ kJ}$ to $80\text{ kJ}$ provides a uniform upward shift of $+4\%$ to $+12\%$, with the most pronounced benefit observed in multi-hop optimization configurations where energy reserves were previously binding.

\subsubsection{Response Time Analysis}
Figure~\ref{fig:rt_mixed} presents the end-to-end response time decomposed into physical Onboard Task Execution Time ($T_{exec}$, solid lower bar) and Network/Queuing Delay ($T_{net+wait}$, opaque upper bar).

The empirical results demonstrate a clear architectural bifurcation in latency dynamics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heuristic Invariance:} The distributed heuristics maintain remarkably flat response times across all traffic rates, bounded tightly between $0.70\text{ ms}$ and $0.80\text{ ms}$ under \textit{Std} and reaching at most $0.85\text{ ms}$ under \textit{VL-heavy}. Crucially, $T_{exec}$ remains invariant at $0.30\text{--}0.34\text{ ms}$, while $T_{net+wait}$ accounts for $0.40\text{--}0.50\text{ ms}$. Because distributed heuristics execute immediate local forwarding without ingress buffering queues, their network delay reflects pure physical ISL propagation and transmission time.
    \item \textbf{Hierarchical Latency Inflation:} \texttt{ILP-Hierarchical} models suffer substantial latency expansion under load. Mean response times rise from $0.80\text{--}0.85\text{ ms}$ at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$ to $1.15\text{--}1.28\text{ ms}$ at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$. The decomposition reveals that while onboard task processing time on the destination SEN ($T_{exec}$) increases modestly from $0.33\text{ ms}$ to $0.48\text{--}0.52\text{ ms}$ under concurrent core utilization, the primary growth driver is $T_{net+wait}$ (expanding to $0.70\text{--}0.76\text{ ms}$), reflecting queuing backlog during hierarchical task aggregation.
    \item \textbf{Low-Latency Bounds of Fast Dispatch:} The centralized fast-dispatch models (\texttt{BS20, BT0.05}) achieve the lowest baseline response times in the entire benchmark, recording just $0.55\text{--}0.62\text{ ms}$ at low load—substantially faster than distributed heuristics. Under high load ($\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$), response time expands moderately to $0.68\text{--}0.75\text{ ms}$. Their onboard execution footprint is exceptionally low ($T_{exec} \le 0.26\text{ ms}$), while capping the batch timeout at $0.05\text{ s}$ suppresses ingress queuing accumulation.
    \item \textbf{Small-Batch Mapping Discrepancy:} Under small batching (\texttt{BS2, BT2}), Energy mapping displays an artificially low response time ($0.42\text{--}0.65\text{ ms}$) because it aggressively drops tasks requiring multi-hop routing, admitting only low-delay single-hop assignments. Conversely, Time mapping balances both components ($T_{exec} \approx 0.22\text{--}0.25\text{ ms}$, $T_{net+wait} \approx 0.40\text{--}0.48\text{ ms}$), delivering an aggregate latency of $0.62\text{--}0.73\text{ ms}$ while sustaining substantially higher completion rates.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Failure Diagnostics}
Figure~\ref{fig:rej_mixed} illustrates the diagnostic distribution of task rejections across arrival rates, battery budgets, and payload distributions. The failure taxonomy uncovers a fundamental dichotomy between heuristic and mathematical programming paradigms:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Heuristic Hardware Bottlenecks:} For distributed heuristics (\texttt{DTS-base}, \texttt{DTS-Optimal}, \texttt{OrbitAware}), task drops are entirely governed by physical hardware saturation, with \textit{Deadline Exceeded} accounting for $<1\%$ of drops across all scenarios. Instead, rejections are split between \textit{Insufficient CPU} ($55\text{--}70\%$, green) and \textit{Insufficient CPU+NET} ($30\text{--}45\%$, orange). Because these heuristics make instantaneous routing decisions without holding tasks in buffer queues, drops occur exclusively when candidate SEN CPU cores or egress transceivers are saturated.
    \item \textbf{Optimization Buffer Timeout Dominance:} For \texttt{ILP-Hierarchical} models, \textit{Deadline Exceeded} (blue) represents the overwhelming source of failure, accounting for $65\text{--}85\%$ of all rejections across all arrival rates. Ingress buffering and hierarchical coordination delays consume the slack time, causing tasks to expire before solver dispatch can be completed.
    \item \textbf{Mitigation via Fast-Dispatch:} In centralized fast-dispatch configurations (\texttt{BS20, BT0.05, Time}), truncating the ingress queue window reduces \textit{Deadline Exceeded} drops to $45\text{--}55\%$. This allows physical capacity drops (\textit{Insufficient CPU} at $20\text{--}25\%$ and \textit{Insufficient CPU+NET} at $25\text{--}30\%$) to re-emerge as active rejection mechanisms, reflecting an operating regime much closer to the physical boundary of the constellation.
    \item \textbf{Small-Batching Dynamics (\texttt{BS2, BT2}):} Small-batch configurations exhibit a sharp divergence governed by the optimization objective. Under Time mapping (bar~[8]), \textit{Deadline Exceeded} drops decrease from $\approx 25\%$ at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$ to $5\%$ at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$, as higher arrival rates fill the $BS = 2$ threshold almost instantaneously and eliminate buffering dwell times, shifting rejections toward physical capacity limits (\textit{Insufficient CPU} and \textit{Insufficient CPU+NET}). Conversely, under Energy mapping (bar~[9]), deadline violations remain closer to $0\%$ across all arrival rates, with rejections split almost exclusively between \textit{Insufficient CPU} ($60\text{--}65\%$) and \textit{Insufficient CPU+NET} ($35\text{--}40\%$), since the energy objective proactively discards high-cost multi-hop assignments before deadline constraints become active.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Energy Inelasticity and Success Rate Dynamics}
Figure~\ref{fig:sr_cpu} illustrates the task completion rate across the arrival rate spectrum $\lambda \in [2, 10]\text{ req/s}$ under $40\text{ kJ}$ and $80\text{ kJ}$ battery budgets, evaluated across the four payload profiles $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ for the purely computational profile $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0, 1, 0)$.

The most prominent finding emerging from this scenario is the complete \textbf{inelasticity to energy budget scaling}. Comparing the $40\text{ kJ}$ and $80\text{ kJ}$ profiles reveals virtually identical success curves for each evaluated algorithm. Because the tasks carry negligible data volume and there are no requests belonging to the CPU+Data class, optical inter-satellite transmission incurs marginal power consumption, rendering battery energy reserves a non-active constraint. The invariance of the curves across the four $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$ subplots (Std, L-heavy, VL-heavy, and Pure L) further confirms that payload size does not impact system performance in any way.

Under these operating conditions, a marked performance differentiation is observed among algorithmic paradigms:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Resilience of Distributed Heuristics:} The heuristic algorithms (\texttt{DTS-base}, \texttt{DTS-Optimal}, and \texttt{OrbitAware}) demonstrate remarkable robustness, recording completion rates of $88\text{--}89\%$ at light load ($\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$) under $40\text{ kJ}$ (climbing to $94\text{--}95\%$ at $80\text{ kJ}$) and degrading in a gradual, linear manner down to $72\text{--}74\%$ at $40\text{ kJ}$ (and $76\text{--}77\%$ at $80\text{ kJ}$) at the saturation limit ($\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$). Their greedy evaluation without upstream queuing allows them to directly saturate the physical capacity of SEN cores without incurring artificial delays.
    \item \textbf{Collapse of Hierarchical Formulations:} The \texttt{ILP-Hierarchical} models (for both Time and Energy metrics) suffer an immediate breakdown as soon as traffic exceeds $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$. The success rate drops precipitously from $70\text{--}76\%$ at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$ down to $46\text{--}48\%$ at $\lambda = 3\text{ req/s}$, before stabilizing at $25\text{--}26\%$ under $40\text{ kJ}$ and $28\text{--}30\%$ under $80\text{ kJ}$ at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$. Combinatorial complexity and multi-tier aggregation delays render the hierarchical paradigm inadequate for handling dense computational workloads.
    \item \textbf{Divergence in Centralized Configurations:} The centralized orchestrator with fast-dispatch (\texttt{ILP-Centr [BS20, BT0.05, Time/Energy]}) maintains good resilience under light traffic ($76\text{--}77\%$ at $40\text{ kJ}$ and $84\text{--}85\%$ at $80\text{ kJ}$), but progressively degrades to $29\text{--}30\%$ at $40\text{ kJ}$ and $32\text{--}33\%$ at $80\text{ kJ}$ under full load. Conversely, the small-batching configuration with latency optimization (\texttt{ILP-Centr [BS2, BT2, Time]}) demonstrates superior resilience under peak computational stress, sustaining a completion rate of $46\text{--}47\%$ at $40\text{ kJ}$ and $57\text{--}58\%$ at $80\text{ kJ}$ at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$. Its energy-optimizing counterpart (\texttt{ILP-Centr [BS2, BT2, Energy]}) remains penalized at $37\text{--}38\%$ under $40\text{ kJ}$ and $46\text{--}47\%$ under $80\text{ kJ}$ due to its tendency to select multi-hop routes that are suboptimal for latency.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Response Time Analysis}
Figure~\ref{fig:rt_cpu} illustrates the end-to-end response time decomposition between the physical execution time on the SEN cores ($T_{exec}$, solid lower bar) and the queuing/network propagation delay component ($T_{net+wait}$, opaque upper bar) under the $40\text{ kJ}$ and $80\text{ kJ}$ budgets.

The analysis highlights a pronounced divergence in system latency behavior:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Invariance of Heuristics:} Distributed heuristic algorithms maintain extremely stable and flat response times across the entire arrival spectrum, bounded between $1.03\text{ ms}$ and $1.08\text{ ms}$ under both $40\text{ kJ}$ and $80\text{ kJ}$. The $T_{exec}$ portion remains strictly fixed at $\approx 0.45\text{ ms}$, while $T_{net+wait}$ accounts for $\approx 0.58\text{--}0.62\text{ ms}$. Because forwarding decisions are instantaneous, no upstream queue accumulation occurs, and the waiting time coincides solely with physical transit over optical links.
    \item \textbf{Latency Explosion in Hierarchical ILP:} The \texttt{ILP-Hierarchical} models exhibit severe total latency expansion, climbing from $1.13\text{--}1.15\text{ ms}$ at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$ to peaks between $1.45\text{ ms}$ and $1.55\text{ ms}$ at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$. This increase is driven both by a modest rise in onboard task execution time ($T_{exec}$ rising from $0.50\text{ ms}$ to $\approx 0.65\text{--}0.70\text{ ms}$ due to processor core contention on the SEN) and, above all, by the inflation of $T_{net+wait}$ ($\approx 0.80\text{--}0.85\text{ ms}$) caused by task dwell times during hierarchical cluster aggregation.
    \item \textbf{Latency Containment in Fast-Dispatch:} Centralized fast-dispatch configurations (\texttt{BS20, BT0.05}) exhibit the lowest absolute latency under light traffic ($0.68\text{--}0.73\text{ ms}$), expanding to $1.00\text{--}1.05\text{ ms}$ at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$. The $50\text{ ms}$ timeout constraint prevents tasks from dwelling prolonged periods in ingress buffers, curbing $T_{net+wait}$ relative to hierarchical solutions.
    \item \textbf{Invariance to Energy Doubling:} Latency metrics remain perfectly superimposable between the $40\text{ kJ}$ and $80\text{ kJ}$ conditions, further confirming that temporal dynamics are driven solely by CPU contention and scheduling queues.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{The Fundamental Rejection Dichotomy}
Figure~\ref{fig:rej_cpu} illustrates the diagnostic breakdown of task rejection causes. The results confirm the existence of a profound structural dichotomy between forwarding heuristics and mathematical optimization models:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Distributed Heuristics (\texttt{DTS-base}, \texttt{DTS-Optimal}, \texttt{OrbitAware}):} Exactly \textbf{$100\%$ of all rejections} are caused by \textit{Insufficient CPU} (green) across all simulated conditions. The rejection rate for \textit{Deadline Exceeded} is identically equal to $0\%$, and drops due to network saturation (\textit{Insufficient CPU+NET} at $0\%$) and orbital eclipse (\textit{Sunset} at $0\%$) are likewise entirely absent. Because heuristics operate without accumulation buffers, a task is rejected exclusively when candidate node processors have exhausted their available physical cores, without any artificial scheduling delay intervening.
    \item \textbf{Mathematical Optimization Models (\texttt{ILP-Hierarchical}, \texttt{ILP-Centralized}):} Conversely, the vastly predominant cause of rejection is \textbf{\textit{Deadline Exceeded}} (blue). In the \texttt{ILP-Hierarchical} configurations, the share of deadline expirations escalates from $40\text{--}65\%$ at $\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$ up to \textbf{$75\text{--}80\%$ at $\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$}, relegating \textit{Insufficient CPU} to a marginal $20\text{--}25\%$. In centralized fast-dispatch variants (\texttt{BS20, BT0.05}), \textit{Deadline Exceeded} reaches $85\text{--}90\%$ under peak load, while \textit{Insufficient CPU} accounts for the remaining $10\text{--}15\%$.
\end{itemize}

This behavior demonstrates that under elevated computational intensity, the expansion of queue waiting times consumes task slack time before the solver can dispatch requests to the executing nodes. Requests fail not due to ISL link unavailability or constellation energy depletion (the \textit{Insufficient CPU+NET} metric is identically $0\%$), but because cumulative latency renders the temporal constraint $R_{r,i} \le D_r$ mathematically infeasible. Finally, the absolute invariance of the diagnostic shares between $40\text{ kJ}$ and $80\text{ kJ}$ confirms that the system bottleneck is purely temporal and computational, and uncoupled from onboard energy storage capacity.

\begin{enumerate}

\end{enumerate}    
\item \textbf{Eclipse Mitigation Robustness:} Rejections triggered by orbital dusk (\textit{Sunset}, brown) remain strictly at \textbf{$0\%$} throughout all tests, verifying that the multi-objective penalty weight ($\gamma^* = 8$) continues to completely shield data-intensive transfers from line-of-sight eclipse interruptions.
\end{itemize}

\section{Trade-Off Analysis and Operational Discussion}
\label{sec:bench_tradeoffs}

Correlating the empirical findings across the nine evaluated scenarios—spanning arrival intensities $\lambda \in [2, 10]\text{ req/s}$, energy budgets of $40\text{ kJ}$ and $80\text{ kJ}$, four payload profiles $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$, and three workload classes $\boldsymbol{\beta}$—reveals four foundational operational trade-offs governing satellite task offloading.

\subsection{Theoretical Global Optimality vs. Ingress Queuing Latency}
\label{subsec:tradeoff_optimality_queuing}

The fundamental architectural dilemma of centralized satellite orchestration lies in the trade-off between global combinatorial scheduling efficiency and ingress queuing latency:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Distributed Heuristics:} Operate on an immediate dispatching model with zero buffer aggregation. Requests arriving at an Access Point (AP) are evaluated instantaneously against local node states and direct ISL hop availability. This reactive posture yields exceptional resilience against heavy request arrival rates ($\lambda \ge 6\text{ req/s}$), preventing deadline expiration entirely ($0\%$ \textit{Deadline Exceeded} across all scenarios). However, lacking global topological visibility, heuristics make greedy, localized forwarding decisions. In data-intensive regimes, this myopic routing causes localized inter-plane link saturation and uneven battery drain across the constellation.
    \item \textbf{Centralized Mathematical Optimization:} Solves an exact Mixed-Integer Linear Program over the full constellation graph. While the solver runtime on the orchestrator is negligible ($T_{solver} \le 0.30\text{ ms}$) and the physical task execution time on the assigned SEN remains bounded ($T_{exec} \le 0.30\text{--}0.45\text{ ms}$), aggregating requests into optimization batches introduces static ingress delay ($T_{wait}$). In compute-intensive and mixed regimes, this buffering time consumes the task deadline slack:
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta t_{slack} = D_r - (T_{wait} + T_{up} + W_{cpu,i} + T_{exec} + T_{down})
    \end{equation}
    When $\Delta t_{slack} < 0$, tasks expire inside the orchestrator queue before assignment can occur, triggering widespread cascade rejections ($60\%\text{--}90\%$ of drops).
    \item \textbf{The Inversion in Data-Intensive Workloads:} Conversely, when computational demands are minimal ($\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0, 0, 1)$), onboard CPU queues ($W_{cpu,i}$) do not accumulate. Under these conditions, the slack margin remains wide enough to absorb batching dwell times, enabling hierarchical ILP to decisively outperform distributed heuristics ($98\text{--}100\%$ vs. $93\text{--}95\%$ completion rate) by coordinating multi-hop optical flows around congested ISLs.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Energy Budget Sensitivity Across Dimensional Boundaries}
\label{subsec:tradeoff_energy_sensitivity}

The empirical evaluation formally demonstrates that fleet battery sizing ($40\text{ kJ}$ vs. $80\text{ kJ}$) exerts a heavily asymmetric impact depending on workload dimensionality:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Compute-Bound Regimes ($\boldsymbol{\beta}=(0, 1, 0)$) — Absolute Energy Inelasticity:} 
    Doubling the usable battery budget from $40\text{ kJ}$ to $80\text{ kJ}$ produces virtually identical completion curves (performance delta $<1.5\%$). Because the transferred payload sizes are negligible, optical transceivers operate at minimal power draw ($P_{net} \cdot S_r / B_{u,v} \approx 0$). The operational bottleneck is governed strictly by onboard processor clock cycles and queue concurrency limits. Consequently, expanding satellite battery capacity yields zero throughput recovery when physical CPU saturation or ingress queuing timeouts dominate.
    \item \textbf{Communication-Bound Regimes ($\boldsymbol{\beta}=(0, 0, 1)$) — High Energy Elasticity:} 
    In sharp contrast, transmitting large payload files ($20\text{--}50\text{ MB}$) across multi-hop ISL paths demands substantial transceiver power. Under a stringent $40\text{ kJ}$ budget, edge nodes rapidly exhaust their operational energy reserves, depressing completion rates to $45\text{--}56\%$ at peak load. Expanding battery reserves to $80\text{ kJ}$ unlocks substantial throughput gains of $+12\%\text{--}18\%$, restoring peak completion rates to $81\text{--}88\%$ for hierarchical ILP and allowing mathematical models to sustain optimal multi-hop paths without triggering premature node exhaustion.
    \item \textbf{Mixed Multi-Service Regimes ($\boldsymbol{\beta}=(0.40, 0.35, 0.25)$) — Intermediate Elasticity:} 
    Heterogeneous workloads exhibit moderate sensitivity ($+4\%\text{--}12\%$ throughput improvement under $80\text{ kJ}$), with benefits concentrating primarily in optimization models that leverage multi-hop ISL routing for large data sub-classes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Fast-Dispatch vs. Controlled Small-Batching Orchestration}
\label{subsec:tradeoff_dispatch_policies}

Benchmarking the two tuned centralized optimization archetypes provides clear design boundaries regarding queue management:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Centralized Fast-Dispatch (\texttt{BS20, BT0.05, Time}):} 
    Enforcing an aggressive timeout ($BT = 0.05\text{ s}$) flushes tasks almost continuously, emulating a pipeline while maintaining global constraint validation. This configuration consistently outperforms small-batching under heavy load ($\lambda \ge 6\text{ req/s}$), bounding response times below $0.75\text{ ms}$ and reducing \textit{Deadline Exceeded} failures from $85\%$ to $45\text{--}55\%$.
    \item \textbf{Controlled Small-Batching (\texttt{BS2, BT2, Time}):} 
    Waiting for a second request ($BS = 2$) provides combinatorial pairing opportunities that yield superior performance at light arrival rates ($\lambda \le 3\text{ req/s}$). However, under heavy load, arrival bursts induce queue accumulation that degrades success rates. Furthermore, coupling small-batching with energy minimization (\texttt{BS2, BT2, Energy}) results in severe path inflation, producing the highest latency spikes in data-intensive regimes ($1.85\text{--}2.25\text{ ms}$) due to detour routing.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary of Chapter Findings}
\label{sec:bench_summary}

The comprehensive comparative benchmark across distributed heuristics, hierarchical ILP, and tuned centralized orchestrators establishes five definitive findings:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Isolation of the Temporal vs. Physical Bottleneck:} 
    In compute-heavy workloads, failure profiles reveal a binary architectural split: distributed heuristics fail strictly due to physical processor saturation ($100\%$ \textit{Insufficient CPU}, $0\%$ \textit{Deadline Exceeded}), whereas optimization models fail overwhelmingly due to temporal deadline expirations ($60\%\text{--}90\%$ \textit{Deadline Exceeded}). Ingress buffering delay erodes task slack time before mathematical dispatching can take place.

    \item \textbf{Dimensional Asymmetry of Energy Sensitivity:} 
    Workload dimensionality governs sensitivity to satellite battery capacity. Compute-bound workloads ($\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0, 1, 0)$) are completely inelastic to energy scaling, yielding identical completion rates under $40\text{ kJ}$ and $80\text{ kJ}$. Conversely, communication-heavy workloads ($\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0, 0, 1)$) exhibit high elasticity, where doubling onboard battery capacity recovers $+12\%\text{--}18\%$ throughput by sustaining transceiver power across multi-hop optical paths.

    \item \textbf{Algorithmic Inversion in Data-Intensive Regimes:} 
    Centralized and hierarchical optimization models are not universally inferior to heuristics under heavy load. In data-intensive scenarios, \texttt{ILP-Hierarchical} decisively outperforms distributed heuristics ($98\text{--}100\%$ vs. $93\text{--}95\%$ at low load, and $81\text{--}88\%$ vs. $77\text{--}78\%$ at peak load). Global network visibility enables the solver to steer large payloads around bottlenecked inter-plane ISLs, whereas greedy heuristics induce localized link congestion.

    \item \textbf{Invariance of Physical Execution Time ($T_{exec}$):} 
    Across all evaluated algorithms, arrival rates, and workload profiles, the physical processing time on destination SEN cores remains strictly stable and bounded ($T_{exec} \le 0.30\text{--}0.45\text{ ms}$). This empirically refutes the widespread assumption that mathematical optimization or onboard hardware execution forms the operational bottleneck; latency growth is driven entirely by ingress queuing and network buffering ($T_{net+wait}$).

    \item \textbf{Absolute Robustness of Eclipse Mitigation:} 
    Across all 9 algorithmic configurations and all parametric sweeps, task rejections due to orbital dusk (\textit{Sunset}) remained strictly at $0.0\%$. Calibrating the multi-objective penalty factor to $\gamma^* = 8$ provides complete protection against line-of-sight eclipse interruptions while avoiding the circuitous path inflation and $55\%$ latency penalties incurred by over-penalized formulations ($\gamma \ge 1000$).
\end{enumerate}

These empirical findings rigorously define the operational boundaries of centralized orbital orchestration, establishing the foundation for the concluding assessments and future research directions presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusions}.
