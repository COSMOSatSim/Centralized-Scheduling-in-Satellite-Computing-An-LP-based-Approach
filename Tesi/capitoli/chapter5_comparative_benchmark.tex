\chapter{Comparative Benchmark}
\label{chap:comparative_benchmark}

Having formalized the mathematical optimization framework in Chapter~\ref{chap:system_model} and established the calibrated operational parameters in Chapter~\ref{chap:performance_evaluation}, this chapter presents an extensive comparative performance evaluation of the proposed Centralized Integer Linear Programming (ILP) Orchestrator against state-of-the-art distributed heuristics and hierarchical optimization baselines.

Deploying orbital edge intelligence across Low Earth Orbit (LEO) constellations involves balancing competing architectural paradigms[cite: 3, 4]. On the one hand, decentralized heuristics provide rapid, low-complexity decisions with negligible queuing overhead[cite: 3]. On the other hand, optimization models (both distributed and centralized) search the joint combinatorial space of compute allocation and multi-hop Inter-Satellite Link (ISL) routing to achieve theoretically optimal resource utilization[cite: 1, 3].

To quantify the operational trade-offs between these paradigms, this benchmark evaluates 9 algorithm configurations across a comprehensive testing matrix spanning diverse workload profiles, real-time payload dimensions, initial battery energy reserves, and request arrival rates.

\section{Experimental Setup and Baseline Algorithms}
\label{sec:bench_setup}

The comparative evaluation is conducted within the event-driven discrete-event simulation framework developed in Python with \texttt{SimPy}, previously detailed in Section~\ref{subsec:methodology_roadmap}[cite: 4, 7]. The simulation faithfully reproduces satellite orbital kinematics, dynamic line-of-sight visibility windows, optical ISL transceivers, processor queues, and onboard battery charge-discharge cycles[cite: 3, 4].

\subsection{Benchmark Dimensioning and Scenario Parameters}
\label{subsec:bench_parameters}

The orbital network is configured as a regional Walker-Delta LEO constellation operating at an orbital altitude of $600\text{ km}$ with an orbital inclination of $53^\circ$. Adjacent satellites are interconnected via full-duplex optical ISLs characterized by nominal transmission bandwidths of $B_{u,v} = 1\text{ Gbps}$ and dynamically computed propagation latencies $\tau_{u,v}$[cite: 4]. Computational requests generated by Ground Users (GUs) are ingested through regional Ground Access Points ($AP_{global}$) and forwarded to candidate Satellite Edge Nodes (SENs)[cite: 4].

The benchmark explores a multi-dimensional parameter space:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Workload Distribution Vector $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (\beta_G, \beta_C, \beta_{CD})$:} Represents the proportion of Generic tasks, CPU-intensive tasks, and Data-intensive tasks[cite: 4]. We systematically evaluate three distinct operational regimes:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textit{Mixed Multi-Service Profile:} $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0.40, 0.35, 0.25)$, representing a balanced heterogeneous mission[cite: 4].
        \item \textit{Computation-Heavy Profile:} $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0, 1, 0)$, stressing onboard processor clock cycles[cite: 1, 4].
        \item \textit{Communication-Heavy Profile:} $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (0, 0, 1)$, stressing ISL link capacity and data transfer energy[cite: 1, 4].
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Real-Time Payload Vector $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (\alpha_M, \alpha_L, \alpha_V)$:} Governs the distribution of input/output data payload sizes across Medium ($1\text{--}5\text{ MB}$), Large ($5\text{--}20\text{ MB}$), and Very Large ($20\text{--}50\text{ MB}$) files[cite: 4]. Four payload profiles are benchmarked:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textit{Standard (Std):} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0.30, 0.50, 0.20)$[cite: 4].
        \item \textit{Large-heavy (L-heavy):} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0, 0.70, 0.30)$.
        \item \textit{Very-Large-heavy (VL-heavy):} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0, 0.30, 0.70)$[cite: 1].
        \item \textit{Pure Large (Pure L):} $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = (0, 1.0, 0)$[cite: 1].
    \end{enumerate}

    \item \textbf{Fleet Operational Energy Budget ($E_{i}$):} Candidate SENs are initialized with usable battery energy reserves of either $40\text{ kJ}$ (stringent energy constraint) or $80\text{ kJ}$ (relaxed energy constraint) per orbital pass[cite: 1, 4].

    \item \textbf{Traffic Intensity Spectrum ($\lambda$):} Request arrival rates vary systematically across $\lambda \in \{2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10\}\text{ req/s}$, transitioning the network from a low-load regime (mean inter-arrival time of $0.50\text{ s}$) to a heavy-load saturation regime (mean inter-arrival time of $0.10\text{ s}$)[cite: 1, 4].

    \item \textbf{Service Deadline Relaxation ($\Delta D$):} In accordance with the macro-tuning findings of Chapter~\ref{chap:performance_evaluation}, the maximum allowable latency threshold incorporates a baseline relaxation factor of $\Delta D = 20\%$ to ensure fair evaluation under heavy traffic[cite: 1, 4].
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Evaluated Algorithms and State-of-the-Art Baselines}
\label{subsec:bench_algorithms}

The comparative suite comprises 9 algorithmic configurations categorized into three architectural paradigms:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Distributed Online Heuristics:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{\texttt{DTS-base} (Dynamic Task Scheduling Base):} A greedy distributed baseline that offloads tasks to the geographically closest available SEN possessing sufficient idle CPU capacity, without coordinating multi-hop ISL traffic[cite: 3, 8].
        \item \textbf{\texttt{DTS-Optimal} (DTS Access-Point Optimized):} An enhanced multi-criteria greedy heuristic that jointly evaluates candidate SEN processing queues and the initial access-point ISL link bandwidth before dispatching tasks[cite: 1, 3, 8].
        \item \textbf{\texttt{OrbitAware}:} A trajectory-predictive distributed heuristic that leverages orbital ephemeris and contact window forecasting to select shortest routing paths and balance computational load dynamically[cite: 3, 8].
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Distributed / Hierarchical Optimization Models:}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{\texttt{ILP-Hierarchical (Time)}:} A two-stage mathematical optimization formulation solved locally within satellite clusters, prioritizing task response time minimization[cite: 1, 3].
        \item \textbf{\texttt{ILP-Hierarchical (Energy)}:} A two-stage distributed formulation that prioritizes constellation energy preservation in the primary optimization phase[cite: 1, 3].
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Proposed Centralized ILP Orchestrator (Tuned Champions):}
    Following the micro-tuning calibration established in Chapter~\ref{chap:performance_evaluation}, the centralized orchestrator is evaluated under its optimal objective weights ($\alpha^* = 1000, \beta = 1.0, \gamma^* = 8$) across its two viable batching archetypes[cite: 1, 4]:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{\texttt{ILP-Centr (BS20, BT0.05, Time)}:} Reactive fast-dispatch configuration with a 50~ms timeout, optimizing for end-to-end response time[cite: 1, 4].
        \item \textbf{\texttt{ILP-Centr (BS20, BT0.05, Energy)}:} Reactive fast-dispatch configuration, optimizing for fleet cumulative energy preservation[cite: 1, 4].
        \item \textbf{\texttt{ILP-Centr (BS2, BT2, Time)}:} Controlled small-batching policy ($BS=2$), optimizing for response time[cite: 1, 4].
        \item \textbf{\texttt{ILP-Centr (BS2, BT2, Energy)}:} Controlled small-batching policy ($BS=2$), optimizing for fleet cumulative energy[cite: 1, 4].
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Comparative Performance Evaluation across Workload Profiles}
\label{sec:comparative_evaluation}

We evaluate the algorithms across three primary performance metrics:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Task Success Rate (\%):} The fraction of total offloaded requests successfully routed, executed on an onboard SEN, and delivered to the requesting ground station within the deadline $D_r$ without violating battery limits[cite: 7].
    \item \textbf{End-to-End Response Time (ms):} Decomposed into \textit{System Execution Time} ($T_{exec}$, capturing solver resolution and local CPU processing) and \textit{Network/Wait Time} ($T_{net+wait}$, capturing batch queuing, ISL propagation, and transmission)[cite: 1, 4].
    \item \textbf{Rejection Causes Breakdown (\%):} The diagnostic distribution of task drops categorized into \textit{Deadline Exceeded}, \textit{Insufficient CPU+NET}, \textit{Insufficient CPU}, \textit{No Server Found}, \textit{ILP Infeasible}, and \textit{Sunset}[cite: 1, 4].
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Success Rate Dynamics}
Under low arrival rates ($\lambda = 2\text{ req/s}$), all distributed heuristics (\texttt{DTS-base}, \texttt{DTS-Optimal}, \texttt{OrbitAware}) achieve exceptional completion rates exceeding $94\text{--}96\%$[cite: 1]. The proposed centralized orchestrator with fast-dispatch (\texttt{ILP-Centr [BS20, BT0.05]}) demonstrates competitive throughput, reaching $74\text{--}78\%$, while the small-batching configuration (\texttt{ILP-Centr [BS2, BT2]}) achieves $80\text{--}84\%$ under Energy Mapping[cite: 1].

As traffic intensity escalates toward heavy-load stress ($\lambda = 10\text{ req/s}$), the distributed heuristics exhibit superior resilience, maintaining completion rates between $78\%$ and $83\%$[cite: 1]. Conversely, optimization-based approaches experience throughput degradation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{ILP-Hierarchical} models drop to $38\text{--}42\%$ under $40\text{ kJ}$ battery budgets and recover modestly to $43\text{--}46\%$ under $80\text{ kJ}$[cite: 1].
    \item The centralized fast-dispatch variants (\texttt{BS20, BT0.05}) sustain completion rates of $40\text{--}44\%$, outperforming hierarchical formulations[cite: 1].
    \item The small-batching policy (\texttt{BS2, BT2}) declines to $30\text{--}36\%$, driven by buffer queuing accumulation during traffic bursts[cite: 1].
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Response Time Analysis}
Figure~\ref{fig:rt_mixed} highlights a clear structural divergence in latency components[cite: 1]. Distributed heuristics achieve remarkably flat response times across all arrival rates, bounded within $0.68\text{--}0.78\text{ ms}$[cite: 1]. Because heuristics make instantaneous forwarding decisions, their queuing delay is virtually zero[cite: 3].

In contrast, optimization models display latency expansion under heavy load:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \texttt{ILP-Hierarchical (Time)} incurs significant network and waiting inflation, with average response times climbing from $0.80\text{ ms}$ at $\lambda=2\text{ req/s}$ to $1.15\text{--}1.25\text{ ms}$ at $\lambda=10\text{ req/s}$[cite: 1].
    \item Centralized configurations with fast dispatch (\texttt{BS20, BT0.05}) effectively contain latency growth, maintaining mean response times below $0.72\text{ ms}$[cite: 1].
    \item Importantly, the pure computational execution time ($T_{exec}$) for the centralized ILP solver remains invariant and strictly bounded ($<0.30\text{ ms}$), verifying that the branch-and-cut routine in HiGHS does not suffer from algorithmic stalling[cite: 1, 3]. Latency growth is dominated entirely by the ingress buffering term $T_{net+wait}$[cite: 3].
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Failure Diagnostics}
Figure~\ref{fig:rej_mixed} reveals the physical nature of task drops in the mixed regime[cite: 1]. Rejections for distributed heuristics are split evenly between \textit{Insufficient CPU} ($55\text{--}65\%$, green) and \textit{Insufficient CPU+NET} ($35\text{--}45\%$, orange)[cite: 1]. Because heuristics do not buffer requests, drops occur strictly when physical hardware or link transceivers are saturated[cite: 1, 3].

Conversely, in the ILP-based models, \textit{Deadline Exceeded} (blue) emerges as a substantial failure cause, accounting for $40\text{--}65\%$ of all drops[cite: 1]. This confirms that mathematical optimization models drop tasks primarily because ingress waiting time erodes the slack time required to complete execution within $D_r$[cite: 3]. Finally, orbital \textit{Sunset} drops remain strictly at $0\%$ across all configurations, validating the robustness of the calibrated penalty $\gamma^* = 8$[cite: 1, 4].

The CPU-intensive profile induces severe performance differentiation[cite: 1]. In sharp contrast, all optimization models experience steep degradation once traffic exceeds $\lambda = 4\text{ req/s}$:

\subsubsection{The Fundamental Rejection Dichotomy}
Figure~\ref{fig:rej_cpu} presents the most profound architectural insight of the benchmark[cite: 1]. We observe a complete, binary divergence in failure causes between heuristics and optimization formulations:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Distributed Heuristics (\texttt{DTS}, \texttt{OrbitAware}):} Exactly \textbf{$100\%$ of all task rejections} are caused by \textit{Insufficient CPU} (green bars)[cite: 1]. Because heuristics evaluate nodes greedily, if a satellite's CPU core is occupied, the task is dropped immediately without waiting[cite: 1, 3]. Consequently, not a single task fails due to \textit{Deadline Exceeded}[cite: 1].
    \item \textbf{Mathematical Optimization Models (\texttt{ILP-Hierarchical}, \texttt{ILP-Centralized}):} Between \textbf{$60\%$ and $88\%$ of all rejections} are caused by \textbf{\textit{Deadline Exceeded}} (blue bars)[cite: 1]. Under heavy computational demands, processor queues ($W_{cpu,i}$) expand rapidly across all candidate satellites[cite: 3]. When the ILP solver aggregates tasks, the mathematical constraint:
    \begin{equation}
        R_{r,i} = T_{up}(r,i) + W_{cpu,i} + \frac{d_{cpu,r}}{C_{sen,i}} \le D_r - T_{wait}
        \label{eq:cpu_deadline_constraint}
    \end{equation}
    becomes infeasible for a majority of candidate nodes[cite: 3, 4]. Tasks fail not because the constellation lacks physical transceivers, but because cumulative queuing delay violates the rigid application deadline[cite: 3, 4].
\end{itemize}

\section{Trade-Off Analysis and Operational Discussion}
\label{sec:bench_tradeoffs}

Correlating the empirical findings across the 9 evaluated scenarios synthesizes four foundational operational trade-offs governing satellite task offloading[cite: 1, 3].

\subsection{Theoretical Global Optimality vs. Ingress Queuing Latency}
\label{subsec:tradeoff_optimality_queuing}

The central dilemma of centralized satellite orchestration lies in the trade-off between global scheduling efficiency and ingress queuing latency[cite: 3, 10]:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Distributed Heuristics:} Operate on an $M/M/1$ or $M/G/1$ queue with zero buffer aggregation[cite: 3]. Tasks are evaluated immediately upon arrival at the gateway Access Point[cite: 3]. This responsiveness yields exceptional resilience against heavy request arrival rates ($\lambda > 6\text{ req/s}$), preventing deadline expiration[cite: 1, 3]. However, lacking global visibility, heuristics make greedy, localized decisions that cause uneven battery drain and cannot avoid downstream link congestion[cite: 3].
    \item \textbf{Centralized ILP:} Solves an exact combinatorial problem over the entire constellation graph[cite: 3, 4]. While the solver runtime is negligible ($T_{exec} \le 0.30\text{ ms}$), the necessity of buffering requests to form batches introduces static ingress delay ($T_{wait}$)[cite: 1, 3, 4]. In CPU-intensive scenarios, this waiting time consumes valuable deadline slack, triggering cascade rejections[cite: 1, 3, 4].
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Energy Budget Sensitivity Across Computational Dimensions}
\label{subsec:tradeoff_energy_sensitivity}

Our results demonstrate that battery reserve sizing ($40\text{ kJ}$ vs. $80\text{ kJ}$) exerts an asymmetric impact depending on workload dimensionality[cite: 1]:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Compute-Bound Regimes ($\boldsymbol{\beta}=(0, 1, 0)$):} Inelastic to energy scaling[cite: 1]. The system bottleneck is governed strictly by the CPU clock frequency ($C_{sen,i} = 1\text{ GHz}$) and maximum concurrent task limits[cite: 1, 3]. Adding battery reserves does not prevent processor queue overflow[cite: 1].
    \item \textbf{Data-Bound Regimes ($\boldsymbol{\beta}=(0, 0, 1)$):} Highly elastic to energy scaling[cite: 1]. Moving 50~MB payloads across 4 ISL hops consumes several kilojoules of transmission energy[cite: 1, 3]. Expanding battery capacity from $40\text{ kJ}$ to $80\text{ kJ}$ unlocks substantial throughput gains ($+15\text{--}25\%$), enabling sustained multi-hop routing[cite: 1].
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Fast-Dispatch (\texorpdfstring{$BT=0.05\text{ s}$}{BT=0.05 s}) vs. Controlled Small-Batching (\texorpdfstring{$BS=2$}{BS=2})}
\label{subsec:tradeoff_dispatch_policies}

Comparing the two centralized archetypes validates the critical importance of dispatch responsiveness[cite: 1, 4]:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The \textbf{Fast-Dispatch configuration ($BS=20, BT=0.05\text{ s}$)} consistently outperforms small-batching under heavy load ($\lambda \ge 6\text{ req/s}$)[cite: 1, 4]. By enforcing an aggressive 50~ms timeout, the coordinator flushes tasks almost instantaneously, effectively emulating an online pipeline while retaining mathematical constraint checking[cite: 1, 4].
    \item The \textbf{Controlled Small-Batching configuration ($BS=2, BT=2.0\text{ s}$)} excels at low traffic ($\lambda \le 3\text{ req/s}$), where waiting for a second task provides the solver with multi-request optimization opportunities without risking deadline expiration[cite: 1, 4].
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary of Chapter Findings}
\label{sec:bench_summary}

The comprehensive comparative benchmark establishes four definitive conclusions[cite: 1, 3]:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Validation of Fast-Dispatch Centralized Scheduling:} The tuned centralized orchestrator operating under fast-dispatch ($BS=20, BT=0.05\text{ s}$) maintains competitive completion rates ($74\text{--}78\%$ at low traffic, $40\text{--}44\%$ under stress) while strictly bounding solver computation latency ($T_{exec} < 0.30\text{ ms}$)[cite: 1, 4].
    \item \textbf{Isolation of the CPU Deadline Bottleneck:} In compute-heavy regimes, optimization-based failures are overwhelmingly driven by temporal deadline expiration ($60\text{--}88\%$ \textit{Deadline Exceeded}), whereas heuristic failures stem strictly from physical hardware limits ($100\%$ \textit{Insufficient CPU})[cite: 1].
    \item \textbf{Energy Elasticity in Communication Regimes:} Data-intensive workloads reward global mathematical routing, enabling ILP formulations to achieve up to $100\%$ success rates when supported by sufficient battery reserves ($80\text{ kJ}$)[cite: 1].
    \item \textbf{Complete Elimination of Sunset Loss:} Across all 9 evaluated configurations and hundreds of simulation runs, connection drops due to orbital eclipse remained at exactly $0\%$, confirming the absolute efficacy of the calibrated multi-objective sunset penalty ($\gamma^* = 8$)[cite: 1, 4].
\end{enumerate}

These empirical findings define the operational boundaries of centralized orbital orchestration, providing the foundation for the concluding assessments and future research directions presented in Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusions}[cite: 3, 4].
